## Physical Deep Dive
:::deep-dive Liquid cooling does not erase the air load
### Follow where the heat ends up
“Liquid cooled” describes part of a heat path, not its final destination. Schneider Electric distinguishes liquid-to-air systems, which reject captured heat into the room, from liquid-to-liquid systems connected to facility water or a dedicated rejection system. That distinction matters before assigning any relief to the existing room cooling. A cold plate can cool a processor effectively while the room plant still carries its heat downstream. [Schneider's cooling architecture guide · August 21](https://www.se.com/za/en/about-us/newsroom/news/press-releases/Designing-the-modern-AI-cooling-stack-6a88100a8492c63fef087105/)

### Work the same rack two ways
Consider a hypothetical 100 kW rack. Assume 80 kW enters the liquid loop and 20 kW remains an air load. These are invented values for arithmetic, not a specification for an Equinix site or any vendor platform.

With a liquid-to-liquid coolant distribution unit, the 80 kW transfers into facility water. The room must still remove the remaining 20 kW, plus relevant local equipment losses. Across ten identical racks, that residual becomes 200 kW. An impressive liquid-system rating does not make that air requirement disappear.

Now replace that unit with a liquid-to-air heat exchanger located in the same room. The captured 80 kW returns to room air. Together with the rack's direct 20 kW air load, the room again receives approximately 100 kW, before additional losses. The liquid path addresses heat removal at the chip; it has not exported that heat beyond the room's cooling boundary.

### Use the boundary in the delivery review
Today's OData announcement describes a hybrid air/liquid installation. It does not disclose enough engineering detail to assign the hypothetical percentages above to those sites. Its relevance is the need to account for both paths rather than assume one replaces the other.

For a delivery review, ask for a simple heat balance showing rack input, liquid export, residual air load and exchanger destination. Then reconcile it against the equipment selection and operating cases. Account for network equipment and other loads outside the modeled racks. Ask engineering which assumptions change with workload mix or unavailable cooling equipment. The practical takeaway: establish where the heat crosses the facility boundary before calling liquid cooling a capacity upgrade.
:::

## Logical Deep Dive
:::deep-dive Read the metric before reading the chart
### A number needs a measurement type
A gauge represents a current value that can rise or fall: coolant temperature, pressure or instantaneous flow. A counter accumulates events or quantities and ordinarily increases until it resets. Prometheus documents this distinction explicitly. Applying the wrong calculation can turn good sensor data into a false operating story. [Prometheus metric types](https://prometheus.io/docs/concepts/metric_types/)

For a hypothetical cooling dashboard, “flow = 120 litres per minute” is a gauge. “Total volume = 12,000 litres” is an accumulated quantity. They may describe the same pipe, but they answer different questions. The first describes current flow; the second needs a time interval before it tells you how quickly fluid moved.

### Work one short interval
Suppose the total-volume counter increases from 12,000 to 12,600 litres in five minutes. The simple interval average is 600 divided by five: 120 litres per minute. That does not prove flow stayed at 120 throughout the interval. A dip followed by higher flow could produce the same total, so it cannot replace finer-grained observations when examining a brief interruption.

Now suppose the device restarts and the counter returns to zero. Naively subtracting samples across the restart produces negative flow. A reset-aware counter calculation handles that discontinuity differently from a gauge calculation. Prometheus recommends rates for counters and distinguishes them from snapshots of state. Check the actual exporter and meter behavior rather than assuming every accumulating instrument behaves identically. [Prometheus instrumentation guidance](https://prometheus.io/docs/practices/instrumentation/)

### Make the integration review concrete
Imagine two dashboard teams receive a point called “cooling_flow.” One treats it as an instantaneous rate; the other calculates a rate of change. Both charts may look plausible, but one might be displaying acceleration of flow rather than flow itself. A label alone cannot resolve the ambiguity.

Ask for the quantity, unit, sensor location, sample interval and reset behavior alongside the point name. Demonstrate a normal interval and a device restart in a safe test dataset. Confirm that the exported definition survives each transformation into the historian and dashboard. This advances the earlier stale-data lesson: fresh data can still be interpreted incorrectly. The takeaway is to validate the meaning and calculation together, not merely whether a number arrives.
:::

## PgPM Growth
- **Limit starts to protect completion** — Work in progress includes started work waiting for review, not just work someone is actively touching. DORA recommends matching limits to capacity and helping resolve downstream constraints instead of launching more tasks whenever someone becomes free. For a PgPM, make the review queue visible and agree what happens when it fills. Count blocked items honestly; moving them to a hidden list does not release the commitment. This is an applied organization practice, not a demand to keep every person constantly busy. [DORA · WIP limits](https://dora.dev/capabilities/wip-limits/)
- **Make the batch independently useful** — Smaller batches shorten feedback only when they reach a meaningful review or use point. DORA warns against splitting work and then regrouping everything before testing. In a data-center program, an independently reviewable package can expose an interface problem earlier, but it must retain the evidence and integrated tests needed for the actual system. Small does not mean incomplete, and a document-flow technique is not permission to fragment a safety-critical commissioning sequence. [DORA · Small batches](https://dora.dev/capabilities/working-in-small-batches/)

### Worked example · Stop feeding the same review queue
Hypothetical Hall A has twelve cooling documentation packages open. Two engineering reviewers can each handle one package at a time. Starting four more packages does not create review capacity. The PgPM proposes a visible review limit of two and works with the team to resolve the oldest blocked package before adding another.

Instead of waiting for a combined twelve-package handover, the team finishes one independently reviewable interface package, obtains feedback and applies the correction to subsequent packages. System-level acceptance remains separate. Compare elapsed time and rework before and after the trial; do not announce a productivity improvement merely because fewer cards are visible. The useful leadership move is negotiating a shared pull rule, not demanding faster individual effort.
