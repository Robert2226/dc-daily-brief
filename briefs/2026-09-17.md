## Physical Deep Dive
:::deep-dive A megawatt rating does not describe the first few seconds
### Follow the imbalance
A generator can have enough continuous capacity and still fail to accept a sudden load. Immediately after electrical demand rises, the engine cannot instantaneously supply matching mechanical power. Rotating energy briefly supplies the difference and shaft speed can fall, changing frequency. The excitation system also has to support voltage. A successful transition therefore depends on the size and timing of the step, the starting operating point, and the response of both the source and the load. Cummins' technical paper describes transient performance in terms of departures from nominal conditions and recovery, rather than just a nameplate. [Cummins transient-performance guidance](https://mart.cummins.com/imagelibrary/data/assetfiles/0058629.pdf)

This is especially relevant when a site's generation is part of normal operation. A campus may avoid a utility-capacity constraint yet acquire a different integration problem: its local source must tolerate the customer's changing demand. Planned capacity, steady operation and dynamic response are separate engineering claims. None can substitute for the others.

### The load can resist the voltage dip
A resistive load bank and a regulated electronic load need not behave alike. For a fixed resistance, power is proportional to voltage squared. If voltage drops to 90% of nominal, that load consumes approximately 81% of its original power. That temporary reduction helps relieve the source. A load attempting to maintain constant power instead draws more current as voltage falls, within its operating limits. Caterpillar explains why passing a resistive-load test cannot automatically prove acceptance of the same nominal constant-power load. Actual UPS behavior may change when a current limit or another protection boundary is reached. [Caterpillar's load-type comparison](https://www.cat.com/en_US/by-industry/electric-power/Articles/White-papers/constant-power-loads.html)

Use a simplified hypothetical Hall A example. A balanced three-phase load requires 1 MW at 480 V and unity power factor. Current is approximately 1,000,000 divided by the product of the square root of three and 480: about 1,203 A. At 432 V, maintaining that same power requires about 1,336 A, an 11% increase. This calculation ignores losses, harmonics and protective limits. It illustrates the direction of the interaction; it is not a prediction of a particular UPS or generator.

### Shape the transition, not just the final load
Suppose Hall A has four hypothetical 500 kW groups waiting to transfer, plus battery recharge demand. Applying all four together creates a different event from sequencing them with measured recovery between steps. Recharge can add a further load just when the source is settling. A coordinated design might use a controlled UPS input ramp, a permitted recharge limit or staged starts. The settings must come from an engineered operating envelope, including abnormal states. Caterpillar's gas-generation discussion identifies load sequencing and UPS ramp times as ways to match a data center's profile to generation response. [Generation and load-profile guidance](https://www.cat.com/en_MX/by-industry/electric-power/Articles/White-papers/converting-data-centers-from-diesel-to-gas-power-generation.html)

Staging also has a cost. The energy buffer must support the load while it waits, and a delayed group may miss a service requirement. A successful ramp therefore has to satisfy both electrical behavior and the time available to transfer. Slowing every transition indefinitely is not a complete design.

### Ask for a trace that answers the decision
For this hypothetical integration review, request the pre-event loading, equipment available, step size, load characteristics and time-aligned voltage, frequency, current and power traces. Include recovery and the next step, not merely the lowest voltage. Test the agreed cases through qualified engineering and commissioning procedures; a production experiment is not implied. A configuration that succeeds with every generator available may behave differently during maintenance.

The PgPM's contribution is to expose a mismatch between the evidence and the promise. If a supplier demonstrates a gradual resistive ramp but the service depends on an abrupt electronic load, the missing evidence is specific and resolvable. The next decision should concern that gap, with an engineering owner and an agreed representative test, rather than another repetition of the MW rating.

:::

:::recall Why might two loads with the same rated MW produce different generator transients?
Their behavior during a voltage change can differ. A resistive load draws less power as voltage falls; a regulated constant-power load can draw more current to maintain power until it reaches a limit. Step timing, starting load and the actual control settings also matter.

## Logical Deep Dive
:::deep-dive Protect useful inference by deciding what enters the queue
### Start with the user's deadline
Inference capacity is a service rate under particular conditions. It changes with the model, request sizes, batching, hardware and concurrent work. When incoming work exceeds that rate, accepting every request does not create more capacity. It creates a queue, and eventually requests can finish after they have lost their value. Admission control decides which work enters the constrained part of the system. Google's SRE guidance discusses overload handling through service criticality and throttling; the design objective is to preserve useful service when demand is excessive. [SRE overload guidance](https://sre.google/sre-book/handling-overload/)

For a conversational assistant, the time to the first useful response may matter more than total batch throughput. For an overnight classification job, delayed completion may be acceptable. Those workloads can share infrastructure, but they need not share the same queue or priority. Define what can be deferred and what must fail promptly before measuring the system's peak rate.

### Work through a short surge
Imagine Hall A hosts a simplified inference service that completes 100 equal-sized requests per second. It receives 140 per second for ten seconds. Ignoring startup effects, 400 requests accumulate. Even if arrivals then fall to 80 per second, only 20 requests per second of spare capacity remains to drain the backlog, so clearing it takes another 20 seconds. The burst is over while its consequences continue.

At a service rate of 100 per second, 400 requests ahead of a new request represent roughly four seconds of waiting before its own execution. A two-second end-to-end target is already out of reach. This is an illustrative fluid calculation, not a sizing formula for variable-length language-model requests. Long prompts, long outputs and prefill/decode contention can make the real queue much less uniform. Load tests need the actual request mix.

### Batching spends some latency to buy throughput
Combining compatible requests can improve hardware utilization. NVIDIA Triton's dynamic batcher illustrates the tradeoff: it can wait briefly for additional requests and exposes queue limits, priorities and timeouts. Its batching delay is a scheduling control, not an end-to-end response guarantee. Time already spent at an API gateway, waiting behind occupied model instances, executing the model or sending the response still counts against the user's budget. The documented dynamic batcher is intended for stateless models; a language-model serving engine may use different scheduling mechanisms. [Triton batching and queue policies](https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/triton-inference-server/user-guide/docs/user_guide/batcher.html)

In the hypothetical service, reserve part of the deadline for execution and transport, then decide how much waiting is affordable. A queue limit alone is insufficient when request costs vary. An implementation might also limit concurrent work or estimated token demand. These are design choices to test, not universal values to copy from another installation.

### Make rejection survivable
An overloaded service can reject new work early, defer background jobs, or offer a smaller permitted task. Each changes the product experience. A downgraded model is useful only if its output still meets the application's requirements. Preserve the original request identity and make the chosen outcome explicit to the caller.

Retries must respect the same capacity boundary. If every rejected request returns immediately, the attempted load rises while capacity stays fixed. Google's cascading-failure guidance recommends bounded retries, randomized backoff and retry budgets, and warns about multiplying attempts across layers. A client should stop when the request's overall deadline expires. [SRE retries and deadlines](https://sre.google/sre-book/addressing-cascading-failures/)

For acceptance, graph successful completions within the deadline, rejection rate, queue age and recovery time together. A low latency chart based only on accepted requests can hide aggressive rejection; a high completion count can hide uselessly late results. Run a controlled overload rehearsal that includes the return to normal traffic. The useful contract is how the whole service behaves through the surge and recovery, not just how fast the accelerator runs while the queue is convenient.

The accumulated queue still needs spare service capacity to drain. If the server completes 100 requests per second and new arrivals remain at 80, only 20 per second clears the backlog. Retries can reduce or eliminate that recovery margin.

## PgPM Growth
- **Lesson 1 · Use comparable outcomes to challenge the inside forecast** — Applied practice. Reference-class forecasting starts with completed, sufficiently similar work and asks where the proposed estimate sits among actual outcomes. For an established PgPM, its value is an independent perspective on a polished schedule. Define comparability before inspecting the answer: scope, approval environment, integration novelty, starting gate and completion criterion all matter. Keep the slow cases and explain exclusions. Then compare the distribution with the team's task-based forecast and investigate disagreement. A small or poorly matched sample deserves a wide uncertainty statement, not a confident percentile. The PMI study offers empirical support for this approach; it does not make every historical sample representative. [PMI research on reference-class forecasting](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/practical-application-empirical-evaluation-rcf-10277)
- **Lesson 2 · Aim independent assurance at the decision that could destroy value** — Strategic governance. Assurance should test whether the evidence supports a consequential commitment. For Hall A, the central uncertainty might be the generator's response to the proposed load, or whether an inference service recovers from a surge. Review effort should follow that uncertainty and the consequence of being wrong. Give a reviewer enough independence to challenge the promised date, access to original evidence, and a precise decision to inform. Separate facts, assumptions and recommendations in the result. A reviewer should not become the unacknowledged owner of the delivery plan; the accountable leader still makes and records the decision. APM's toolkit explicitly calls for adapting assurance to the project's circumstances. [APM assurance assessment toolkit](https://www.apm.org.uk/resources/find-a-resource/assurance-assessment-toolkit/)

### Worked example · Hall A's eight-week integration promise
Suppose a hypothetical team forecasts eight weeks from equipment-ready to accepted service. Six genuinely comparable historical integrations took 8, 9, 10, 12, 14 and 17 weeks using those same boundaries. Their median is 11 weeks. Eight weeks is possible in this small sample, but it sits at the optimistic edge; it is not established as a dependable commitment. These are invented teaching values, not Equinix performance data.

The team argues that a preconfigured inference platform saves three weeks. An independent reviewer discovers that the historic delay mostly arose from electrical integration and retesting, rather than platform installation. The claimed saving may be real but may not move the critical finish. The reviewer therefore asks for the load-step evidence and the integrated rehearsal sequence, instead of requiring every workstream to produce a larger slide pack.

The sponsor can fund an early integration test and retain a forecast range while reserving the commercial service date until that evidence is available. If the new result supports a shorter duration, update the forecast and record why this project differs from the reference class. Do not add an arbitrary contingency on top of every existing risk allowance: reconcile what each allowance already covers. The outcome is a more defensible decision, with a specific route to reducing uncertainty.
