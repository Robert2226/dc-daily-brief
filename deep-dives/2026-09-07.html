01Physical · Redundancy depends on failure boundaries

Deep dive

Redundancy depends on failure boundaries

Advance from capacity to availability

Yesterday's lesson calculated the cooling available at a stated operating point. Today, use that verified capacity to ask what survives a specific failure. Imagine Hall A needs 900 kW of cooling and has three modules, each capable of 500 kW under the assumed conditions. Two modules can carry the load, leaving one spare. The familiar N+1 label describes this component count. It does not describe every pipe, power source or control dependency needed to deliver that cooling to the load.

Uptime Institute distinguishes redundant components from the distribution paths and operating characteristics needed for concurrent maintainability or fault tolerance. A component count alone cannot establish a Tier classification. Use that distinction to ask a more precise question: which activity or failure can this design tolerate while continuing its intended service? The answer must include the path to the customer load, not just the equipment inside one package.

Background · Tier characteristics

Work through three states

In the normal state, total nominal capacity is 1,500 kW. Remove one module and 1,000 kW remains, above the 900 kW load. Now imagine one module already isolated for maintenance and a second module becoming unavailable. Only 500 kW remains. The 400 kW shortfall is a consequence of this hypothetical state, not evidence that N+1 was incorrectly counted. The design's promised operating envelope must say whether that combined maintenance-and-failure case is required.

Capacity is only the first part of the example. Assume all three modules depend on one common pump. Losing that pump may interrupt the cooling service despite having healthy modules. Adding a fourth module does not remove the pump dependency. Alternatively, independently supplied modules might still connect through one section of pipe that cannot be isolated without stopping flow to the row. Trace the complete useful function, from energy supply through heat transport to the load, to locate the limiting boundary.

Draw the shared dependency

A simple dependency sketch can reveal more than a list of spare assets. In our imagined arrangement, three modules connect to a common distribution segment before reaching Hall A. Draw that shared segment as a single box. Annotate what happens when it is unavailable and which loads lose service. Repeat for control power and any supervisory function the local equipment actually needs. Do not assume loss of a dashboard stops a controller; verify the dependency rather than inventing one.

Uptime's close-coupled cooling discussion specifically calls attention to what happens to an aisle when a coolant distribution unit is removed or fails. That is the level of analysis a useful drawing enables: a spare elsewhere may not reach this affected load. The lesson also transfers to today's fiber story, where nominally separate services can share a physical route.

Background · Cooling distribution and reliability

Turn the model into a delivery conversation

For this fictional project, ask the engineering team to describe normal service, planned isolation, and the agreed failure cases. Ask operations how access, spares and restoration time affect those states. Ask the integrator whether a common control dependency changes the physical conclusion. These questions should converge on one service model; separate equipment schedules can otherwise hide the same shared element from every package owner.

The PgPM contribution is to make the tradeoff explicit. An independent path may cost more initially but reduce a maintenance constraint. A spare module may be inexpensive yet leave the dominant dependency intact. Commissioning evidence should demonstrate the agreed scenarios using the approved engineering test plan, with observed service behavior and stated limits. This classroom example is not a live switching procedure or a proposed Equinix design.

Does a fourth cooling module remove the shared-pump failure in this example?

No. It adds module capacity, but every module still depends on the same pump. The mitigation must address the dependency that interrupts the useful cooling service, and the required maintenance and failure states must be stated separately.

02Logical · Read access is not control authority

Deep dive

Read access is not control authority

Follow a permission, not just a measurement

The last two editions followed an alarm and then delayed or repeated telemetry. Today, imagine that Hall A's monitoring integration works correctly. A historian records measurements, a DCIM application uses them with asset and capacity information, and a DCOS dashboard presents operational context. The next request is to let a remote specialist investigate a problem. The key design question becomes what that identity can do after connecting, especially whether it can alter a controller's behavior.

Start with a hypothetical boundary: monitoring applications may read approved measurements; an operator application may perform specified supervisory actions; local controllers retain their defined control functions. These are proposed responsibilities for the example, not universal product features. BMS, SCADA, a historian and a DCIM tool can be integrated without receiving identical privileges. Today's microgrid story makes this distinction tangible because orchestration can involve commands with physical effects.

Related news · Microgrid controls

Separate three decisions

Network reachability determines whether traffic can reach an endpoint. Authentication establishes the identity accepted by that endpoint. Authorization determines which operations that identity may perform. An allowed network connection does not settle the other two. If reading and writing use the same permitted service, opening its port alone does not make the connection read-only. The application or an appropriate intermediary must enforce the required operation boundary.

OPC UA's role model gives a concrete illustration. A server evaluates the permissions associated with roles granted to a session for the requested node and operation. Its examples distinguish browsing, reading and writing. Permissions from multiple applicable roles are combined, so adding a broader role can undermine an intended restriction. A reassuring account name such as “viewer” is therefore weaker evidence than checking its effective rights. Implementations still need qualification; a standards feature is not proof that a particular deployment is configured correctly.

Protocol background · Evaluating role permissions

Work an access failure scenario

Suppose the remote specialist receives the same account used by the commissioning tool. The dashboard still looks read-only, but the account has permission to change setpoints through another client. Hiding an edit button has not removed the underlying capability. In this imagined review, record the required investigation operations, assign a distinct identity and test both an allowed read and a prohibited write in a suitable test environment. Check the event record as well as the screen result.

Now suppose remote access is revoked. The team must understand what stops: the specialist's session, data collection, or the local control function. Those are different outcomes. An arrangement that unintentionally requires a remote identity service for essential local operation has introduced a new dependency. Describe the expected loss behavior before adding the security component, then test against that expectation. This connects directly to the physical lesson's question about shared dependencies.

Make the boundary maintainable

NIST's OT security guide emphasizes security measures that account for operational performance, reliability and safety. Its remote-access guidance includes managed access and the ability to disconnect remote access without disrupting OT operations. Apply that principle through a site-specific design: a controlled access point, appropriately limited privileges, accountable sessions and a defined way to end access. The exact architecture belongs with the responsible engineering and security teams.

Background · NIST SP 800-82r3, September 2023

For handover, the durable artifact is a small matrix connecting identity, destination, permitted operation and expected behavior when access is removed. Include who maintains that mapping when vendors or software versions change. The cost of keeping permissions accurate belongs in the support model, alongside patching and equipment maintenance. Today's cloud connector release demonstrates the same reasoning in another domain: a convenient authentication path does not establish the right authorization model for every audience.

Related news · Knowledge-base access
Why is a read-only dashboard insufficient evidence of read-only access?

The display can hide controls while the underlying identity still has write privileges through another client or interface. Verify effective permissions at the enforcing system and test denied operations as well as successful reads.

03PgPM Growth · Shared risk and lifecycle cost

Lesson 1 · Evaluate shared risk across package boundaries

At the strategic-judgment level, look beyond separate red, amber and green package reports to the dependency that can affect several outcomes together. NASA's technical risk guidance links a clear risk statement to consequences, likelihood, mitigation and monitoring. Apply it here by describing a specific service loss and the shared dependency behind it. In hypothetical Hall A, mechanical, controls and network packages can each meet their own deliverables while depending on one support path. Your recommendation should identify the combined exposure, the decision needed and the residual risk after the proposed treatment. This advances earlier dependency coordination by asking whether individually successful projects still leave a concentrated program risk.

Lesson 2 · Compare alternatives across the operating life

At the cross-functional business-case level, compare options against explicit technical, cost and schedule criteria, then test which uncertain assumptions change the recommendation. NASA's decision-analysis process provides that structure. First identify mandatory requirements; an option that cannot meet them is not made acceptable by a low price. For viable alternatives, bring installation, support and restoration implications into one comparison. Yesterday's pilot lesson asked where evidence transfers. Today asks whether the chosen solution remains attractive when the operating team inherits its recurring work. State the decision horizon and the assumption most worth verifying, rather than hiding uncertainty inside a single precise total.

Worked example · Hall A's support alternatives

Assume two technically acceptable support arrangements address the required shared-dependency cases. Option A costs $100,000 initially and $30,000 per year to operate; Option B costs $140,000 initially and $10,000 per year. Over three years, the simplified undiscounted totals are $190,000 and $170,000. B's $40,000 initial premium is offset by $20,000 annual savings after two years. These invented figures illustrate a method; they exclude discounting, taxes, energy differences, residual value and uncertain incident costs, so they are not a full lifecycle estimate.

Now challenge the input that drives the recommendation. If B's operating cost is actually $20,000 annually, its three-year total becomes $200,000 and A is lower at $190,000. The next useful work is to substantiate the support effort, not add decimal places to the original estimate. Separately, if A does not meet a mandatory independence requirement, the cost ranking cannot waive it. Present both the technical constraint and the sensitivity to the decision owner.

Daily Action · One dependency, one cost assumption

Optional 8-minute private exercise: sketch a shared dependency in a hypothetical project, name the outcomes it can affect, and write one cost assumption that could reverse an alternatives comparison. End with the evidence you would seek. No messages or system changes are needed.

Does the lower three-year total decide the preferred option?

Only after both options meet the mandatory requirements and the comparison's assumptions are credible. The sensitivity example reverses the ranking, showing why support effort needs evidence before the recommendation is treated as robust.
