Translate electrical ratings into usable capacity

Start with the quantity being constrained

A hypothetical Hall A expansion is described as having “two 10 MVA transformers” and “12 MW available.” Those statements might both be correct, yet neither establishes how much IT load can be supported. Megavolt-amperes describe apparent power; megawatts describe real power. Power factor is real power divided by apparent power. At a load power factor of 0.95, a 10 MVA apparent-power limit corresponds to 9.5 MW of real power, before considering other limits. Eaton’s explanation is a useful reference for this relationship and for checking both UPS ratings.

Power factor and UPS ratings

This is not a statement that five percent of the electricity is wasted. Power factor and conversion efficiency are different concepts. A device can have a high input power factor and still incur conversion losses. In a program review, confusing these quantities can produce an apparently precise capacity calculation that subtracts the wrong thing.

Work through an illustrative envelope

Assume the utility import limit is 12 MW. Each transformer can carry 10 MVA under the specified conditions, the aggregate load power factor is 0.95, and the design must support the required load with either transformer unavailable. Assume the distribution can actually transfer that load to the remaining transformer. Under those assumptions, the one-transformer real-power limit is 9.5 MW. The two nameplates sum to 20 MVA, but that sum does not describe the required degraded operating state.

Now assume the facility needs 1.5 MW for cooling and other supporting loads at the design condition. A simplified allowance for IT is 9.5 minus 1.5, or 8 MW. This ignores any additional reserve and assumes all relevant losses are included in the supporting-load figure. If a downstream UPS path permits only 7.5 MW of IT load, that smaller limit governs. You cannot restore the missing half-megawatt by citing the utility agreement or transformer total.

Energy efficiency is a different view

Power Usage Effectiveness, or PUE, divides total facility energy by IT equipment energy over the same boundary and period. The Green Grid’s definition is intentionally about energy. A favorable annual PUE does not prove adequate electrical or cooling headroom during the hottest design condition.

PUE definition

For example, a hypothetical annual total of 12,000 MWh divided by 10,000 MWh of IT energy gives PUE 1.2. That tells you the annual supporting-energy overhead was 2,000 MWh. It does not tell you whether a particular afternoon requires 1 MW or 2 MW of cooling power. The Department of Energy’s design guide explains how annual measurement captures changing operating conditions and why PUE describes supporting infrastructure rather than the usefulness of the computation.

DOE design guide · 2024, section 8

Ask what changes when the load changes

Return to Hall A. If the relevant aggregate power factor were 0.90 instead of 0.95, the same 10 MVA limit would correspond to 9 MW. With the same illustrative 1.5 MW supporting load, only 7.5 MW remains before other constraints. This is sensitivity analysis, not a recommendation to install correction equipment. The electrical designer must establish actual load characteristics, harmonics, temperature derating and protection requirements.

The PgPM’s useful contribution is to make the comparison consistent. Ask each discipline to describe the same phase, operating state and boundary. A capacity statement should explain which limit is currently controlling and which evidence supports it. The answer may change after a transformer outage, a cooling-season change or a customer hardware substitution. Treat capacity as an operating envelope with explicit assumptions, and commercial or schedule discussions become much easier to ground in engineering reality.

Two 10 MVA transformers are installed. Does that establish 20 MW of resilient IT capacity?

No. Apparent power differs from real power; the required failure state may leave only one transformer available. Facility overhead, downstream limits and the actual distribution path further constrain IT capacity.

What does a storage copy actually preserve?

Begin with the recovery claim

Suppose Hall A’s reporting application uses a database whose data files and transaction log occupy separate volumes. The team wants a rehearsal environment before a migration. “We copied both volumes” sounds reassuring, but it leaves three questions unanswered: were they captured at a compatible point, can the database recover them, and can the copy be used without contacting production systems? A storage operation and a successful application rehearsal are different layers of evidence.

A point-in-time block copy preserves the storage state exposed by its mechanism. It does not necessarily include application data still buffered in memory. AWS’s snapshot guidance distinguishes written volume data from cached data and discusses pausing writes or unmounting where appropriate. That documentation is about snapshots; do not assume every clone product has identical consistency guarantees. Establish the guarantees of the selected operation explicitly.

AWS snapshot consistency guidance

Follow a transaction through the gap

In a hypothetical transaction, a database records the information needed for recovery and later updates the data pages. A recoverable storage image may therefore require log replay before the database reaches a usable state. “Crash-consistent” means the image can resemble a sudden interruption; it does not promise that no recovery work is necessary or that every recent application request was committed.

PostgreSQL documents a concrete example: a consistent filesystem snapshot can be used while the database runs, but recovery requires the write-ahead log, or WAL. It warns that a database spread across filesystems needs simultaneous snapshots for that approach. This is why independently copying the data volume at one moment and its log at another cannot simply be declared a valid backup.

PostgreSQL filesystem backup guidance

For the rehearsal, invent a known test record with an agreed committed timestamp. After recovery, verify the record through the application’s normal read path. Also check a transaction boundary relevant to the application, such as whether related records agree. This does not prove every possible data property, but it gives the test an observable result beyond “the volume mounted.” A database process that starts can still contain the wrong dataset for the intended test.

Separate permissions, location and data handling

Today’s AWS release allows cross-account volume copies, but requires the same Availability Zone and has specific encryption-key sharing rules. Those details make it a useful example of how independence has several dimensions. A destination account may have different administrators while remaining in the same physical failure location. Re-encryption changes key control; it does not redact customer names or secrets inside the data.

Cross-account clone boundaries

In the hypothetical Hall A rehearsal, the copied configuration might still contain production service addresses, scheduled export jobs or notification destinations. Starting it without isolation could cause a test system to send real traffic. Define the rehearsal boundary before startup: test identities, permitted connections, disabled outbound jobs and a known test dataset. These are requirements for the example, not instructions to manipulate a live environment.

Measure the time until useful recovery

An instant copy can still require attachment, database recovery, configuration changes and application verification. If copying takes one minute and recovery plus checks takes forty minutes, reporting a one-minute recovery time describes only the storage step. Measure the interval that matches the service objective, including the work necessary to trust and use the result.

Finally, state what the rehearsal does not demonstrate. A same-zone copy test can prove a repeatable restoration workflow without proving survival of a zone-wide failure. A sanitized dataset can support functional testing without reproducing production scale. Make those limits visible so a successful narrow test earns the right conclusion. That precision lets an established PgPM compare proposals by the evidence they produce and avoid turning a convenient copying feature into an unsupported recovery promise.

Related AWS storage release
The cloned database starts in another account. Have you proved recovery from an Availability Zone failure?

03PgPM Growth · Turn disagreement into a defensible choice

Resolve competing interests · Ask what each position protects

At the cross-functional leadership level, a disagreement often contains useful information about different consequences. An Operations lead asking for more rehearsal may be protecting recovery confidence; a delivery lead asking to proceed may be protecting a customer commitment. State each concern in terms its owner recognizes, then separate factual disagreement from different preferences. APM’s conflict guidance supports addressing these differences directly and constructively. Your role is to make the tradeoff discussable without dismissing either person’s expertise or treating their first proposed solution as their only acceptable outcome.

APM: constructive conflict

Agree criteria before ranking options · Find the uncertainty that could change the choice

Move beyond the earlier meeting and decision-record lessons by asking whether the parties agree on what makes an option acceptable. Separate required conditions from preferences, then compare feasible alternatives against the same criteria. NASA’s decision-analysis guidance connects the value of more analysis to whether uncertainty can change the ranking. In a delivery review, a disputed test duration may deserve investigation if it changes the recommended option; another decimal place in an immaterial estimate may not. Make the authorized decision-maker’s tradeoff explicit instead of allowing a score to conceal it.

Worked example · A rehearsal with three legitimate concerns

In hypothetical Hall A, Delivery wants to keep Friday’s migration window, Operations wants a recovered-database demonstration, and Security wants production data kept out of an insufficiently protected test environment. Treat those as interests to satisfy, not personality labels. The initial proposals are “skip the rehearsal” and “copy everything into test.” Neither necessarily meets all three needs.

The team agrees on required conditions: no unauthorized production data exposure, a successful application-level recovery demonstration, and a documented migration decision before Thursday noon. They then compare a third option: a protected, representative rehearsal with approved data handling and outbound integrations disabled. Assume its preparation takes four hours and execution two hours, with six hours available. It is feasible in the simple schedule, but has no margin.

The decisive uncertainty is whether data preparation really fits four hours. If evidence suggests six, the plan exceeds the window and needs a changed scope, more time or a different migration decision. An average score for “speed” and “safety” would obscure that threshold. A useful recommendation is: “Choose the bounded rehearsal if preparation can be demonstrated within four hours; otherwise revisit the migration window. The unresolved point is preparation duration, and here is the evidence we need to settle it.” This protects the legitimate concerns while keeping the decision concrete.

Daily Action · Reframe one disagreement

Optional: set an 8-minute timer and write two opposing positions from a hypothetical program, the concern each protects, one shared required condition and a third option. Finish by naming the single missing fact most likely to change your recommendation. Keep the note private; no communications or external changes are needed.
